Test #01 SOLE Voltage

Presentation

Hi, I’m Nicolás, the cacharreo (tinkering) expert at Voltaje. Today, we want to share the design of this User Test that we put together and ran in early 2024. We learned a ton during this process, and we’d love for you to take these ideas, make them your own, and use them to interact with your own communities.

To evaluate the functionality of three connectivity solutions currently documented on the Voltaje platform.

Audience

Profile 1 - Doña Marlén, interested (+), skills (-)

She is over 50 years old, with a genuine interest in knowing how to use the internet to improve her way of life. She finds it difficult to use cell phones, computers, and browse the internet. She is a mother.

Profile 3 – Pepito, interested (-), knowledge (+)

He is between 14-21 years old, a student who likes using social media and playing video games. He likes having the latest tech gadgets and is well-informed about them.

Profile 2 - Paila Gorrea, interested (-), skills (+)

She is 30-40 years old, works with NGOs coordinating projects, and lives between the city and the rural communities she works with. She interacts with large funders to secure financing for NGO projects.

Methodology

We present VOLTAJE along with some categories of interest to us regarding its functionality. Then, we let the guests read and develop a solution individually, while in parallel, they share their observations regarding the presented categories. This is repeated with another solution. At the end of the solution, specific questions are asked regarding the categories of interest, and finally, an open group feedback session is held.

Observer

Your task is to be present, taking careful notes on how the user develops a solution by reading the instructions. You must be attentive to notice points of boredom and frustration.

Moderator

This is the person who presents and directs the final feedback. The other observers can participate by asking occasional questions, keeping the following recommendations in mind:

  • Avoid giving judgments and reflections.
  • Do not give opinions about the solutions.

This doesn’t mean you can’t prompt people for answers.

Since the central categories are “readability and viability,” the conversation will focus on these. The “accessibility” category might be explored in detail at a time when VOLTAJE is more advanced.

Observation during the test

Instructions:

  • Before the guest starts working on the solution, explain your role and why you are there in concrete terms.
  • You can hand the evaluation form to the participant once they have finished the solution or decided to stop.
  • Tell each participant to take their time and read each of the statements they are going to evaluate carefully.
  • 10 minutes before finishing the test, ask the participant to stop their work and fill out the evaluation.

Observer’s role:

  1. Foster a calm and trusting space for each participant. The observer should not judge what the participant is doing. This should be a supportive role.
  2. Take note of the participants’ reactions and behaviors while they work on the solution; without making them uncomfortable, being invasive, or intimidating the participant.
  3. Take note of the questions each participant has and suggest that they do/solve them as they see fit; but without over-explaining, helping, or trying to answer their doubts.

Suggested questions: How would you solve that doubt or problem? What do you think is failing in the explanation or solution that is causing you to have that doubt or problem?

  1. Invite participants to evaluate each of the solutions calmly and honestly. The more objective and critical the participants are, the more useful the session will be.

If you have any doubts about your role as an observer during the test, take note of the doubt. You can only intervene if you see an imminent risk.

Final feedback questions

Reflection on the change in the device for profiling interest, knowledge, needs, and skills

  • Were there any changes in how interested you are in tinkering with gadgets?
  • Was there a change in how much knowledge you think you have?
  • Was there a change in how much skill you think you have?
  • How did you find the experience, how did you feel?
  • How did you feel?
  • How was the timing?
  • What did you find most difficult?
  • What did you find easiest?
  • What did you learn?
  • If you were to do this exercise again, what would you change?

Supporting concepts

Instruction

By the term instructions, I refer to all discourses that order behavior or establish conduct, with imperatives, periphrasis of obligation, enumerations of actions, lists of facts, graphic illustrations, etc. Instructions include cooking recipes, traffic codes, contest rules, appliance user manuals, medication leaflets, employment contracts, or DIY guides. These types of texts govern our lives.

Criteria

Instructions should be:

  • For everyone. For beginners, who know nothing, and for experts, who verify details or solve problems.
  • Complete and precise: They must cover everything: all functions, all steps, all conditions, or all characteristics of the object or task.
  • Appropriate. It is convenient to organize them to “serve” the reader. There are no universal structures or a canonical order. They shouldn’t waste time.
  • Manageable. Besides being understood, each instruction must be operational. The reader must grasp it with one reading and must be able to execute it without difficulty.
  • Neutral. Without emotion, courtesy, irony, or humor.
  • Safe. Confusing instructions can cause accidents.
  • Comfortable: It is convenient to foresee how the instructions will be used and adapt them to the situation.

Recommendations

Below are other tips for writing instructions and recommendations:

Give each group of instructions a title. This can be the objective (installing a program on the PC), the final product (project budget), or the topic (office etiquette rules). The title allows referring to the instructions in other parts of the text.

Choose the most appropriate terms. These must be precise and also simple so that the reader can understand them. Also, ambiguous and confusing formulations should be avoided.

Select the appropriate verb form. It is better to use the imperative verb mode, which expresses a command or exhortation, using the “you” (e.g., avoid the terms) treatment. This way, we address the reader directly. The imperative is clearer than impersonality (e.g., “it is answered”) or the infinitive (e.g., “to answer”). Regarding the treatment (formal or informal), the Writing Manual points out that this will depend on the degree of formality of the text.

  • Start with the verb. Starting each instruction with the verb makes the writing clearer. The first thing the reader sees, at the beginning of each line, is what they must do.
  • Avoid negations, whenever possible. These are harder to understand than affirmations. It is better to formulate instructions affirmatively, if feasible.
  • Eliminate repetitions. Reiterating indications should be avoided. It is better to summarize them.
  • Homogeneity. It is advisable to maintain the chosen writing style in all instructions. For example, it is a mistake to start with a noun -as here, with homogeneity- if a conjugated verb is used in the rest of the instructions. It would be better to say “Write homogeneous instructions.”

Characteristics of an instructional text

  • The sequence of activities is presented in a correct and understandable chronological order, numbering them and using connectors that help the understanding of actions to achieve a result.
  • Graphic marks such as numbers, asterisks, or dashes are used to differentiate or sequence the series of steps.
  • It can be accompanied by graphics, illustrations, and/or drawings, depending on the type of instructional text to be developed.
  • It presents clear, precise, and linear language. It must be understood by anyone.

Guidelines for writing an instructional text

  1. Determine the intention of the text.
  2. Describe the physical resources and functions that make up the action to be performed.
  3. Organize the writing into two parts, headed by a subtitle that identifies its content.
  4. Number each of the steps.
  5. Respect the chronological sequence of the process steps in the writing.
  6. Use verbs in the infinitive, imperative, or 3rd person.
  7. Use chronological connectors: to start, in the first place, in the second place, finally,
  8. then, after, so, starting from, before, next, immediately, etc.
  9. Add some warnings and precautions that the user should keep in mind.
  10. Use appropriate language according to the activity.
  11. Write with clear and simple sentences.

Instructions on how to climb a staircase

Julio Cortázar*

No one will have failed to notice that frequently the floor folds in such a way that one part rises at a right angle to the plane of the floor, and then the next part is placed parallel to this plane, to give way to a new perpendicular, a behavior that repeats in a spiral or broken line up to extremely variable heights. By crouching down and placing the left hand on one of the vertical parts, and the right on the corresponding horizontal one, one is in momentary possession of a step. Each of these steps, formed as seen by two elements, is situated a little higher and further forward than the previous one, a principle that gives meaning to the staircase, since any other combination would produce forms perhaps more beautiful or picturesque, but incapable of moving one from a ground floor to a first floor.

Staircases are climbed facing forward, since backward or sideways they are particularly uncomfortable. The natural attitude consists of standing, arms hanging without effort, head held high although not so much that the eyes stop seeing the steps immediately above the one being stepped on, and breathing slowly and regularly. To climb a staircase, one begins by lifting that part of the body located on the right below, wrapped almost always in leather or suede, and which, except for exceptions, fits exactly on the step. Once said part, which for brevity we will call the foot, is placed on the first step, the equivalent part on the left is picked up (also called the foot, but which should not be confused with the aforementioned foot), and bringing it to the height of the foot, it is made to follow until placing it on the second step, with which the foot will rest on this one, and the foot will rest on the first. (The first steps are always the most difficult, until the necessary coordination is acquired. The coincidence of name between the foot and the foot makes the explanation difficult. Take special care not to lift the foot and the foot at the same time).

Having arrived in this way at the second step, it is enough to repeat the movements alternately until finding the end of the staircase. One exits it easily, with a slight tap of the heel that fixes it in its place, from which it will not move until the moment of descent.
