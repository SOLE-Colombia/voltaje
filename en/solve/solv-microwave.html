¿Cómo conectar dos lugares con un radio enlace?

Instala un puente de internet inalámbrico entre dos puntos distantes usando antenas direccionales.

Hi, I’m David, and this is one of those solutions I finally understood after a lot of exploring, asking around, tinkering, and obsessing over something that seemed impossible at first.

I didn’t arrive at this from a classroom. I got here by asking myself: is there a way to connect two distant places using the air as a path, without having to lay a cable? I still haven’t done it myself — this guide is part of the process of getting ready to try. When I started researching, there was no reliable cellular signal available, and the first answer I got was that it was impossible.

But I explored, asked questions, did my research, and discovered that what seemed impossible actually has a name and is done with two antennas, a bit of patience, and a desire to understand how information travels through the air.

They call it a radio link. And this guide is what I wish I had found from the very beginning.

What is a radio link?

A radio link → is a system that connects two places using radio waves as if they were an invisible bridge in the air.

Imagine you have a very powerful flashlight and you point it directly at another person who has a mirror. The light travels from one to the other without touching anything in between. That’s what a radio link does: instead of light, it uses an internet signal; instead of flashlights, it uses special antennas.

What makes this type of connection special is that the antennas don’t transmit in every direction (like your home WiFi, which is omnidirectional), but rather they focus all their energy toward a single point, like a megaphone that directs your voice exactly where you want it to go. This allows them to reach very far, from a few hundred meters to dozens of kilometers.

Technically, “radio link” is the general name. When high frequencies are used (above 1 GHz), they are also called microwave links. These are the same systems that telecommunications companies use to connect cell towers, but in smaller, more accessible versions for communities.

What is it for?

This solution is for bringing internet from a place that has a connection to one that doesn’t, when the distance between the two makes laying a cable impossible or too expensive.

Think of it this way: if there is fiber optic internet in the town center, and 8 kilometers away there is a rural village that has never had a connection, a radio link can be the bridge that joins those two spaces.

Some of the possibilities it opens up:

  • A single internet connection shared among several families or an entire community, reducing the cost for everyone.
  • Connecting a rural school to the internet without paying for cable infrastructure.
  • Bringing a signal from a high point (an antenna, a tower, a hill) down to homes in the valley.
  • Connecting a community organization’s headquarters with a neighboring antenna that already has a signal.

In Colombia, communities like those in Buenos Aires (Cauca) have built community networks using exactly this principle: one point with internet access that distributes the signal via radio links until it reaches the houses. Buenos Aires - Cauca reference

Before you start…

Warning: this project requires working at heights and handling electrical equipment. If you are going to install antennas on roofs, poles, or towers, make sure to have someone watching from below, wear non-slip footwear, use harnesses and safety equipment (if you have them or can borrow them), and never work alone at heights.

Warning: this solution has an initial cost. The most basic equipment costs between USD 25 and USD 100 depending on the distance you need to cover (approximately 100,000 to 500,000 Colombian pesos). Although the equipment is durable (4–8 years if well cared for), the initial outlay requires planning.

Something to think about: before buying any equipment, do a line-of-sight study (we’ll explain how later). If there is no direct line of sight between the two points, the radio link won’t work and the money will be wasted. That step is free and it’s worth doing first.

What do you need?

Tip: for most community projects in Colombia, the CPE510 is the best starting point. It reaches up to 15 km, works in the 5 GHz band (which has less interference than 2.4 GHz), and its price is accessible. If the distance is less than 5 km or there is a lot of vegetation in the way, the CPE210 on 2.4 GHz might work better.

Each CPE includes in its box:

  • The antenna (the device itself)
  • A passive PoE adapter (the “little box” that powers the antenna with electricity through the network cable)
  • A short network cable

Installation Materials

  • UTP Cat5e or Cat6 network cable — needed from the antenna to the router at each end. The recommended maximum distance is 60 meters per run. Cat6 supports speeds of up to 1 Gbps, which is convenient if you ever have a high-speed connection.
  • 2 poles or masts — one for each end. They can be 2-inch PVC pipes, galvanized iron pipes, or even straight, sturdy wooden posts. The height depends on what is between the two points (trees, buildings, hills).
  • Metal clamps to fix the antenna to the pole. These are usually included with the equipment.
  • Cable ties or plastic zip ties to secure the network cable to the pole.
  • Heat-shrink tubing or self-fusing tape to waterproof the cable connectors outdoors.
  • Drill bit and wall plugs if you need to pass the cable through walls.

Tools

  • Wrench or Allen key to tighten clamps
  • RJ-45 crimping tool and connectors (if you are going to make the cable ends yourself)
  • Multimeter (to check the cable)
  • Bubble level (to install the pole straight)
  • A computer or cell phone to access the configuration interface
  • Compass or a compass app on your phone

Prior Knowledge

  • Knowing how to use a computer and a web browser
  • Comfort with simple work at heights (installing on roofs or low poles)
  • Patience for alignment: it is the most delicate step
  • Someone who has a cell phone at both ends to communicate during installation (or two-way radios)

How to do it?

Step 1 → Study the terrain before buying anything

Before spending a single peso, you need to know if there is line of sight between the two points: that from one end you can see the other directly without anything in the middle.

Radio waves from a point-to-point link cannot pass through hills, buildings, or dense forests. If there is an obstacle in the way, the link won’t work or will work very poorly.

How to verify line of sight:

The fastest way is to physically go to both points and see if you can clearly see the other from one. If you can see them, it will likely work. If you can’t see them, you need to raise the antennas.

You can also do it from home using digital tools:

  1. Open Google Earth Pro (it’s free).
  2. Locate both points on the map.
  3. Use the “Elevation Profile” tool (in the Tools → Ruler menu) to draw a line between the two points and see the terrain profile. If the terrain rises in the middle, there is a problem.
  4. You can also use HeyWhatsThat — a free web tool that shows what you can see from a point at a certain height. It works very well in the United States; in Colombia, you have to do some testing because it isn’t always as accurate as it seems.

The Fresnel Zone: more than just line of sight

Here is something many people don’t know and that costs them dearly: it’s not enough for the two points to “see” each other. Radio waves don’t travel exactly in a straight line like light. They travel in a kind of ellipsoidal bubble called the Fresnel zone.

Imagine throwing a stone into water. The point where it lands creates circular waves that expand. Something similar happens with radio waves: although the “main beam” goes from antenna to antenna, around that beam there is a spherical zone of energy that also needs to be clear.

For the link to work well, at least 60% of the radius of the first Fresnel zone must be free of obstacles. The exact formula is complicated, but the rule of thumb is:

  • For a 5 km link at 5 GHz, the Fresnel zone has a maximum radius of about 5–6 meters at the midpoint of the path.
  • For a 15–20 km link at 5 GHz, that radius rises to about 15–17 meters.

That means if there is an 8-meter tree exactly in the middle of your 5 km link, you must raise your antennas so that the tree stays below the “cone” of the signal.

Tip: use the Fresnel zone calculator from RF Line of Sight Calculator to calculate exactly how much you need to raise your antennas.


Step 2 → Plan the height of the antennas

Once the line of sight is confirmed, decide at what height to install each antenna.

The general rule: the higher, the better, because you reduce the possibility of trees or new buildings blocking the signal in the future. But higher also means bigger, more expensive, and harder-to-install poles.

A practical height for community projects on relatively flat terrain:

  • For distances less than 5 km: 4–6 meters above the highest obstacle in the path.
  • For distances of 5–15 km: 6–10 meters or more, depending on the terrain.

If there are hills in the way, sometimes it’s better to put the antenna on the crest of the hill instead of raising it. This is called a “repeater node” and can be cheaper than a very tall tower.

Something to think about: if you have doubts about the height, erring on the side of higher is less costly than repeating the installation. A taller pole is easier to get than doing everything all over again.


Step 3 → Install the poles or masts

With the heights defined, install the physical supports where the antennas will be.

Support options:

  • 1.5–2 inch galvanized iron pole: The most durable option. It resists wind, rain, and humidity. It can be buried in the ground with cement or bolted to a wall.
  • 2-inch PVC pipe: Cheaper. Works well for heights under 4 meters. At greater heights, it can vibrate in the wind and misalign the antenna. Also, prolonged sun exposure degrades it over time and it can become brittle — if it’s going to be outdoors, consider painting or covering it.
  • Roof mast: A metal structure designed to be anchored to a roof without damaging it. Ideal if the installation is on a house or building.

How to fix the pole:

If it goes into the ground: dig a hole 40–60 cm deep, put the pole in, and fill with cement. Let it dry for 24 hours before mounting anything on top.

If it goes on a wall: use expansion plugs (like Fischer) with stainless steel screws. Stainless steel doesn’t rust in the rain.

Warning: if there are strong winds in your area, add guy wires (ropes or metal rods) to stabilize the pole at various angles. A fallen pole drags the antenna down and can damage it or hurt someone.


Step 4 → Mount the antenna on the pole

Each TP-Link CPE comes with a steel clamp. The process is:

  1. Pass the clamp around the pole.
  2. Hook the antenna onto the clamp (it slides into a slot).
  3. Tighten the screws without tightening them all the way yet — you need to be able to rotate the antenna to align it in the next step.

The antenna has a correct orientation. The TP-Link CPE510 and CPE210 have the label facing forward (where the signal goes). The back (where the LAN port is) faces you when you are behind the pole.

Tip: before taking the antenna up the pole, connect the network cable and turn it on on the ground to verify that it powers up correctly. It is much easier to solve a technical problem down there than while climbing a pole.


Step 5 → Run the network cable from the antenna to the router

The network cable (UTP Cat5e or Cat6) is what carries both the electrical power (via PoE) and the data between the antenna and your network router.

Maximum length: 60 meters per run. If you need more distance between the antenna and the router, you must use a switch or repeater in the middle.

How to run the cable correctly:

  1. Measure the necessary length before cutting — add an extra 20% for bends.
  2. If the cable goes outdoors, secure it every 50 cm with plastic zip ties or cable clips so the wind doesn’t whip it around.
  3. At points where the cable bends (when entering a wall, for example), leave a gentle curve. Sharp bends damage the internal fibers of the cable.
  4. Where the cable enters a building, seal the hole with silicone so water doesn’t get in.

Waterproofing the outdoor connector:

The connection between the cable and the antenna is outdoors. It is one of the points that fails most often over time. Protect it like this:

  • Wrap the RJ-45 connector with self-fusing tape (available at any hardware store), starting from the bottom up so that water runs off.
  • If you want more protection, add a layer of rubber tape on top.

Warning: water entering through the connector is the number one cause of failures in radio links installed in the field. Take your time to waterproof it well. It’s worth it.


Step 6 → Connect the PoE adapter and access the configuration interface

The PoE adapter (the “little box” that comes with the CPE) has two ports:

  • POE: Connects to the antenna (the cable coming from above).
  • LAN: Connects to the computer or your network router.

It also has a power connector to plug into an electrical outlet. The antenna does NOT need direct power — it receives it through the network cable thanks to the PoE.

To access the configuration interface:

  1. Connect the PoE adapter to the computer with a network cable in the LAN port.
  2. Manually set your computer’s IP address to 192.168.0.100 (subnet mask 255.255.255.0).
  3. Open a web browser and type http://192.168.0.254 in the address bar.
  4. The configuration panel will appear. The default username and password are admin / admin (or whatever is on the device label).

Tip: if the CPE doesn’t respond at that address, try 192.168.1.1 or 192.168.0.1. If it still doesn’t work, reset the device to factory settings by holding the RESET button for 10 seconds.


Step 7 → Configure the first end as an “Access Point”

The end that has the internet connection (the one connected to the main router or the provider) is configured as an Access Point (AP).

In the TP-Link CPE interface (Pharos software):

  1. Go to the Wireless section.
  2. Under Mode, select Access Point.
  3. Enter a network name (SSID) — it can be something like community-link-AP. Give it a name you’ll recognize.
  4. Under Frequency, if you are using the CPE510, choose a channel in the 5 GHz band that isn’t being used by other nearby networks. If there are no other networks, any channel works.
  5. Under Security, set a strong password. This link should not be accessible to just anyone.
  6. Save the changes and reboot the device.

Something to think about: the network name (SSID) of the link can be made invisible so it isn’t visible from external devices. You configure this in the “Hide SSID” option. It makes the link more secure in areas with many networks.


Step 8 → Configure the second end as a “Client”

The second device (the one at the remote point, where you want to bring the internet) is configured as a Client.

Repeat the physical connection from Step 6 with the second CPE. Then:

  1. Access the device’s interface in the same way.
  2. Go to Wireless → Mode → Client.
  3. Click Survey. The device will search for available networks.
  4. In the list that appears, select the SSID you gave the first device.
  5. Enter the network password.
  6. Save and reboot.

When both devices are configured, one will automatically search for the other and establish the connection… as long as they are pointing toward each other.

You’re doing well: if you’ve made it this far, you have both devices configured. What’s left is the most exciting part — and also the most delicate: aligning them.


Step 9 → Align the antennas to find the strongest signal

This is the heart of the entire installation. A poorly aligned antenna can have a weak signal or none at all, even if the devices are perfectly configured.

Alignment is done in two stages: first coarse orientation (pointing toward the correct area) and then fine alignment (finding the exact point of maximum signal).

Key tool: The CPE client’s web interface has a section called Signal Strength or Signal Survey that shows the signal level in real-time. You can also use the tpPharOS app on your phone connected to the AP.

The value you are looking for: The RSSI (Received Signal Strength Indicator), measured in dBm. Reference ranges:

RSSI ValueQuality
-45 to -55 dBmExcellent. Very stable link.
-55 to -65 dBmGood. Will work well for most uses.
-65 to -75 dBmAcceptable. There may be drops in heavy rain.
Worse than -75 dBmPoor. Likely instability. You need to check alignment or height.

Step-by-step alignment process:

  1. Communicate with the person at the other end (by cell phone, radio, or hand signals if they are visible).
  2. Orient the client antenna pointing approximately toward the direction where the AP is. A compass or compass app helps.
  3. In the web interface (or app), observe the RSSI value. If you don’t see a signal from the AP, move the antenna slowly in a horizontal arc until you find it.
  4. Once you find a signal, start the fine adjustment: move the antenna very slowly to the right and left, looking for the point where the RSSI is highest (the least negative number).
  5. Fix the antenna at that point and repeat the movement vertically (up and down) to optimize that axis as well.
  6. When you have the maximum on both axes, fully tighten the clamp screws.
  7. The person at the other end does exactly the same with the AP.

Keep it up: alignment can take between 20 minutes and two hours, especially if the two points are very far away or if the terrain is uneven. It’s normal. Every tenth of a dB you gain in RSSI is worth it. Don’t rush this stage.

Tip: if you have two people at each end and they can talk by cell phone, alignment is much faster. One person moves, the other reports the value. You adjust together.


Step 10 → Verify that the link works correctly

With the antennas aligned and the devices running, it’s time to verify that the link has the quality needed for the use you will give it.

From the client’s web interface, go to the Status or Monitor section. Look for these indicators:

RSSI (signal level): Make sure it’s in the green range: between -45 and -65 dBm. You already saw this in the previous step.

CCQ (Client Connection Quality): This is a percentage that indicates the efficiency of the connection. A CCQ of 90% or more is excellent. Below 70%, the link is having trouble transmitting internet packets → correctly.

Real Throughput: Open a browser on a computer connected to the client and test the speed with Fast.com or Speedtest.net. The result should be close (not equal, but proportional) to the speed at the source point.

Something to think about: if the RSSI is good but the speed is low, check if there is interference from other networks on the same channel. Channels are like traffic lanes or different colors: if many networks share the same channel, the space becomes saturated and the data gets trampled. Change the channel in the AP configuration and see if it improves.

Stability test: Leave the link running for 30 minutes and observe if the RSSI remains stable or fluctuates. A stable link should vary by less than ±3 dBm. If the signal goes up and down a lot, it may indicate intermittent obstacles (like trees moving in the wind) or that the alignment isn’t completely fixed.

You’re doing well: if the RSSI is between -45 and -65 dBm, the CCQ is over 90%, and the speed test gives consistent results, you have a functional radio link. That “invisible bridge” you imagined at the beginning now exists.


Recommendations

Heavy rain and thick fog can temporarily degrade the signal, especially in the 5 GHz band. This is normal. If the link only fails during heavy downpours, consider improving the alignment or slightly increasing the transmit power in the device configuration. Keep in mind that increasing power consumes more electricity and can accelerate equipment wear over time.

Connectors and cables outdoors are the most vulnerable point of the installation. Check them every 6 months. Water entering through a small crack can damage the equipment in weeks. Good initial waterproofing is worth much more than repairing or replacing equipment.

If the link has to pass near trees, install the antennas with more margin than you think is necessary. Trees grow. A tree that is 4 meters tall today might be 6 meters in two years and start blocking the signal without you having moved.

Do not mix equipment from different brands in the same link if you can avoid it. TP-Link CPE with TP-Link CPE, Ubiquiti with Ubiquiti. Technically they can work in cross-combinations, but sometimes there are incompatibilities in proprietary protocols that reduce speed or stability.

Document everything: the direction of each antenna in degrees, the RSSI at the time of installation, the height of the mast, the length of the cable. You can do it on video, in a notebook, on a printed piece of paper kept in a plastic bag near the equipment, or on your phone. That record will save you hours if you ever have to check or repeat the installation.

How do you know if this solution works?

Technical signs of success:

  • RSSI between -45 and -65 dBm stably
  • CCQ above 90% constantly
  • Speed test at the remote point with reasonable results (minimum 5–10 Mbps for basic community use)
  • The link stays active for 24 hours without drops

Signs of community impact:

  • People at the remote point can make video calls or watch videos without drops.
  • Children at school can use online educational platforms.
  • The organization can upload photos, documents, or reports without having to travel to the town center.
  • Someone who previously paid for internet separately now shares it more economically with the community.

Other possible internets

A radio link is not just technology. It is a political decision by a community that says: we also have the right to connect, and we are going to build our own way of doing it.

In Mexico, indigenous communities in Oaxaca have been operating their own community telecommunications networks for years using exactly this principle. In the Colombian countryside, projects like those from Colnodo and ISOC have shown that it is possible. You don’t need to be a telecommunications company to build a data bridge between two villages.

You can also explore whether there are telecommunications companies, cooperatives, or entities that can donate equipment or establish a partnership with your community. In Colombia, there are calls from FONTIC and international organizations that support rural connectivity projects — that can reduce the initial cost.

Once you have the link installed, also consider configuring a secure DNS server (like Cloudflare’s 1.1.1.1 or Quad9’s 9.9.9.9) on your network so that browsing is safer and more private for the people who connect.

And if you found this guide useful, you can download or print it to have it available without needing to be connected when you need it most.
