"How to use television for group learning?"

Turn your community television into a space for dialogue and learning with SOLE TV.

Hey there! It’s such a joy to greet you all here. I’ve always believed that television doesn’t have to be just a box that talks at us; it can be a window for us to listen to one another. It happened to me that while watching shows, I’d ask myself: “What if we could answer back, too?“. I’ve learned that when we get together to tinker with the media we already have—like the neighborhood or town channel—magical things happen. I’m no expert in big-budget productions, but I am an expert at sparking good conversations, and today I want to share how you can turn the TV into a giant, fun classroom. Go for it, curiosity is the best engine!

What is SOLE TV?

It’s a proposal to transform television into a Self-Organized Learning Environment (SOLE). It consists of creating television content built by asking the audience to answer a Big Question and then inviting people to send in their responses to share them on a live show. It’s a way for everyone to end up learning together, turning a traditional medium into a network of shared knowledge.

What is it for?

Imagine if you could transform television into a space for communication and learning that doesn’t just go from the channel to the audience, but where the audience participates in creating the TV content and everyone ends up learning together. For places where the internet signal doesn’t reach, or if part of the audience cannot access the internet, using television can extend the reach, especially so that children and young people can navigate and connect to the web.

So, if you have a mass-market or community television channel, you can generate a Self-Organized Learning Environment or SOLE via TV. This allows people in the community, whether they have internet or not, to learn and dialogue through television around Big Questions.

Doing a SOLE TV means creating a new type of television content that is built by asking the audience to answer a Big Question and then inviting people to send in responses to share them on a show. Having many responses allows us to generate a conversation, live, where we are all experts because we build the knowledge together… just like the Internet!

Before you start…

To make your television show a safe and exciting space, remember that coordination is key. You don’t need to be an expert producer, but you do need a lot of willingness to organize the voices of your community.

Attention: Make sure you have the broadcasting permits from the community channel and, if you are going to film minors on the street, always ask for their parents’ consent.

What do you need?

To set up your SOLE TV, gather these tools and get ready with the best energy:

  • A television channel (mass-market, community, or internet-based).
  • A host with lots of energy, spark, and listening skills.
  • An audience willing to participate (at least 2 people to start the chat).
  • A fixed time slot for the show (you can call it SOLE TV).
  • Internet access and/or social media to receive responses (optional).
  • A video recorder or cell phone to capture voices on the street (optional).
  • TV host energy.
  • Lots of curiosity and a desire to learn from what others say.
  • Patience to organize the responses that come in.

How to do it?

Step 1 → Define the Big Question and the show date

Sole Colombia

BIG QUESTION: Define a Big Question that motivates self-organized learning using the available media. The Big Question will be what hooks the audience into participating in this SOLE TV. It should be something that invites investigation and cannot be answered with a simple yes or no.

LIVE SHOW DATE: Define a date so the audience can participate and watch the show; you should call for responses to the Big Question about 15 days in advance to give everyone time to research.


Step 2 → Invite the audience to answer it

Sole Colombia

CALL TO ACTION: Broadcast the Big Question through messages or short commercials on the channel, inviting the audience to answer it via an online form, social media comments, calling the channel, or recording on the street. In the invitation or online form, include the date of the live show and request contact information from those responding so you can reach them directly. Some people might have social media like WhatsApp or Facebook to participate through those means. Others could participate by calling the channel directly or participating in person at the station. You could also collect responses on the street, recording them with video equipment or a cell phone.


Step 3 → Plan the live show

Pexels

SHOW SCRIPT: While you receive the responses to the Big Question, you can organize a simple show rundown and audiovisual bumpers to accompany the live broadcast. For a 1-hour show, it’s worth thinking about how to present the question, invite between 2 and 5 people to give their answers live, and select other audience responses to share.

Prepare a simple script with these moments:

  1. Bumper, greeting, and introduction of guests.
  2. Presentation of the Big Question.
  3. First round of responses (guests and audience).
  4. Invitation to participate via social media or phone.
  5. New questions to dig deeper.
  6. Second round of responses.
  7. Conclusion, thanks, and congratulations on the autonomous learning.

Step 4 → Broadcast the live show

Sole Colombia

SOLE TV: Broadcast a live show using the script as a reference, but allowing for an unstructured conversation combined with other pre-recorded audience responses and/or social media input to solve the Big Question together. Guests can research beforehand. You can also invite people to the show who have knowledge about the answer to share it in the space, or people who are good conversationalists to stir up a great discussion. The host or participants can dig deeper with new questions that arise.

Recommendations

  • Value every response: It doesn’t matter if the answer is short or simple; what’s valuable is the act of participating and building together.
  • Keep it flowing: If the host notices a topic is stalling, they can throw out a “provocative question” to revive the chat.
  • Use what you have: If there is no internet for a form, use a physical ballot box in the town square to collect written responses.

How to know if this solution works?

To verify the success of your SOLE TV, you can observe these technical and social points:

  • Increased participation: Did you receive responses from people who don’t usually participate in the channel? Did messages arrive from distant rural areas?
  • Citizen interaction: Managing to transform television into a Self-Organized Learning Environment (SOLE) and a space for citizen interaction allows communities to learn and dialogue. If you see people still discussing the question at the store or the market, it’s working!
  • Sense of belonging: Does the audience suggest new questions for the next show? That means they already feel like owners of the space.

Other possible internets

Television is a very powerful medium because many people can access it for free. Especially for many children and young people, it may be the only communication device they have access to. It will never be the same as having access to the Internet, as the internet is the largest library in the world. However, television also allows you to connect with many people; the rest lies in them being able to dialogue with the television to learn together. This is what SOLE TV does: turn a mass medium into a bridge of solidarity and shared knowledge.
