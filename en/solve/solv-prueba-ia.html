Using television as a learning tool to do SOLE

What’s it for?

Imagine if you could turn the TV into a space for communication and learning that doesn’t just broadcast at the audience, but actually gets them involved in creating the content—so everyone learns together. In places where the internet signal doesn’t reach, or for folks who can’t get online, using television can extend that reach, especially helping kids and young people explore and connect to the network.

So, if you’ve got a mass-media or community TV channel, you can spark a Self-Organized Learning Environment, or SOLE by TV. This lets people in the community—whether they have internet or not—learn and chat through the TV, centered around Big Questions.

Doing a SOLE TV means creating a new kind of TV show. You build it by asking the audience to answer a Big Question, then inviting folks to send in their answers to share on the show. Having a bunch of answers lets us kick off a live conversation where everyone is an expert because we’re building knowledge together… just like the internet!

What do you need?

  • A TV channel (mass-media, community, or online).
  • A host.
  • An audience (at least 2 people).
  • A time slot for the show (you can call it SOLE TV).
  • Internet access and/or social media (optional).
  • A video recorder or a cell phone (optional).
  • The energy of a TV host.

How to do it?

Define the Big Question and the show date

Sole Colombia

BIG QUESTION: Define a Big Question that sparks self-organized learning using the media you’ve got on hand. The Big Question is the hook that gets the audience to jump into this SOLE TV.

LIVE SHOW DATE: Set a date so the audience can tune in and participate; you should start calling for answers to the Big Question about 15 days beforehand.

Invite the audience to answer

Sole Colombia

THE CALL: Broadcast the Big Question through messages or short promos on the channel, inviting the audience to answer via an online form, social media comments, calling the channel, or by recording answers out on the street. In the invite or online form, include the date of the live show and ask for contact info so you can reach out to them directly. Some folks might have social media like WhatsApp or Facebook to participate that way. Others might prefer calling the channel directly or stopping by the station in person. You could also gather answers out on the street by filming people with video gear or just a cell phone.

Plan the live show

Pexels

SHOW SCRIPT: While you’re getting answers to the Big Question, you can organize a simple rundown and some audiovisual bumpers to go with the live show. For a 1-hour program, it’s worth thinking about:

  • How to present the Big Question?
  • Inviting 2 to 5 people who sent in answers to join the live show and share their thoughts.
  • What other questions could deepen the learning during the live broadcast?
  • Selecting other answers to share during the show.
  • What other ways can we interact with the audience?

Then, put together a simple script for the flow of the show. Here’s a reference:

  1. Intro/bumper, welcome, introduce the guests.
  2. Present the Big Question.
  3. First round of answers between guests and the answers sent in by the audience.
  4. Invite the audience to participate via social media.
  5. New questions to dig deeper.
  6. Second round of answers between guests and audience submissions.
  7. Wrap up the conversation, say thanks, and congratulate the participants on their self-organized learning.

Broadcast the live show

Sole Colombia SOLE TV: Broadcast a live show using your script as a guide, but keep it loose—allow for an unstructured conversation mixed with pre-recorded audience answers and/or social media input to solve the Big Question together. Guests can do some research beforehand. You can also invite people to the show who know a bit about the answer to share their expertise, or just folks who are great conversationalists to stir up a lively debate. The host or participants can dig deeper with new questions that pop up.

What do you achieve?

In many places, there’s no internet signal, but there is TV. Even where there is internet, people might not be able to afford it. So, television becomes a medium that lets people learn with or without the web.

However, TV is usually a one-way street—from the broadcaster to the audience. The audience rarely gets to help build the learning at the station.

Transforming television into a Self-Organized Learning Environment (SOLE) and a space for citizen interaction allows communities without internet (or with limited access) to learn, dialogue, and create thriving communities using TV, or a mix of TV and internet.

How far can you go?

Television is a powerful medium because so many people can access it for free. For many kids and young people, it might be the only communication device they have. It’ll never be the same as having internet access, since the internet is the world’s biggest library and connects you with millions of people across the planet. Still, TV lets you connect with a lot of people, too—the challenge is getting them to talk back to the TV so you can learn together. That’s what SOLE TV does.
