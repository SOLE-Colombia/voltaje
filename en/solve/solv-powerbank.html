Como elegir la bateria correcta

Aprende a calcular la carga real de una batería y elige la mejor opción para tu comunidad.

Hi, I’m David. I’m your tinkerer guide. I enjoy understanding devices by seeing how they work, checking them, and testing them with sincere curiosity. Not long ago, I was filming some videos in the Sierra Nevada where we could only find a power outlet every 3 days. We had to rely on large batteries so the cameras wouldn’t turn off in the middle of the jungle. That’s where I learned that not all batteries are the same and that labels sometimes tell us half-truths.

I once bought a 20,000 mAh battery, excited about not being disconnected on a trip. When it arrived, it was a heavy “brick.” But the worst part was when I tried to charge my phone: it barely gave me 3 or 4 charges. What happened to the rest of the energy? I started investigating and discovered the marketing trick. Here, I’ll tell you how to demystify those numbers so the same thing doesn’t happen to you.

What is the real capacity of a battery?

Almost all batteries claim to have a number in mAh (milliampere-hours), which is like saying how much water a bucket can hold. The problem is that the energy stored inside is at a low “voltage,” and to transfer it to a phone, you have to increase the force. In that process, part of the energy is lost as heat.

Why is it useful to understand this?

Understanding this helps you avoid spending extra money on equipment that promises a lot but delivers little. If you are in a community where the power goes out often or you have to walk a long way to reach a spot with a signal, knowing how much real energy you have in your pocket is the difference between staying connected or being left in the dark.

How do I know if I need it?

  • You feel like your power bank drains very quickly.
  • You are thinking about buying a battery for a laptop and don’t know if it will work.
  • Your community wants to buy a large charging station to share and doesn’t know which one to choose.

How to do it?

Step 1 → Do the “toll” math

Look at the mAh number on the box. To know how much it will actually give you, multiply it by 0.7. If it says 20,000 mAh, you actually have about 14,000 mAh of usable energy. That missing 30% is lost along the way when the battery boosts its force from 3.7V to the 5V your phone needs.

Step 2 → Look for the “High Power” (PD) seal

If you need to charge a laptop, not just any battery will do. Look for one that says Power Delivery or PD and has at least 45W or 65W (watts). Laptops are more demanding, and if the battery is too weak, it simply won’t charge even if it’s plugged in.

Step 3 → Prepare for the sun

If you are going to be away from an outlet for many days, look for a battery that has an input for a solar panel. Some large batteries (called power stations) come ready to be connected directly to a panel that you unfold on the ground or on top of a vehicle.

Step 4 → Check the cables

For heavy-duty work, use thick, original cables. A thin cable is like a clogged hose: the energy doesn’t pass through quickly, and the battery takes an eternity to charge your equipment.

Warning: never leave a battery charging in the sun inside a closed car. The temperature rises so much that the battery can be damaged or, in extreme cases, catch fire.

Don’t be fooled

The market lies to us with numbers so that you buy the option they want. My recommendation is to always look at people’s reviews regarding what you are looking for to solve your energy needs. Often, cheap cables sold on the street don’t let energy pass with the necessary force, or numbers claiming 1 million mAh in the size of your hand are also misleading, so we must think about whether that is really possible. Can you hold a water tank in your hand?

If you need to charge a laptop, look for the battery to say PD (Power Delivery) and be at least 45W or 65W. If it doesn’t have those watts, the laptop won’t even feel that you connected it, and it will drain itself to charge the battery you just plugged in.

What can you achieve with this solution?

You ensure that community work doesn’t stop. Whether it’s filming a documentary, recording data in a rural area, or keeping a community radio station running, these batteries give you the security that the lack of outlets is not an obstacle to resistance and innovation in your territory.
