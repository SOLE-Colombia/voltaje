How to generate electricity using a bicycle?

Generate electricity by building a bike generator.

Hi, I’m Nicolás. I’m what you’d call a “tinkerer.” I’m an engineer, and I’m interested in how things work. I enjoy taking gadgets apart and putting them back together to see what makes them tick, and I also love figuring out how we can use available resources as a community.

What is a pedal generator?

A pedal generator is a system that converts the mechanical energy of a bicycle into electrical energy. Basically, it transforms the pedaling motion into electricity, allowing you to generate power for various purposes, such as charging electronic devices or even powering lighting systems. (Source: Ecologismos)

What is it for?

This solution is a step-by-step guide on how to buy and assemble a bicycle-powered electricity system. We’ve calculated it to keep a computer and a router running for 3 hours, which is the typical duration of a SOLE session.

What do you need?

  • Money to buy batteries, cables, a power inverter, and a controller.
  • Energy to pedal.
  • An old bicycle.
  • Care and plenty of enthusiasm to assemble the system.
  • A stand to keep the bicycle stationary.

How to do it?

Step 1 → Calculate energy consumption

Determine the energy consumption you need. In this case, we need to power a laptop and a router for 3 hours. This will define the capacity our system needs and how many hours we must pedal to generate the necessary electricity.

  • A laptop consumes 120W/h
  • A router consumes 15W/h

Now, we must calculate the energy expenditure required for the system. You can check this information on the back of your devices or on their chargers. For a SOLE session, for example, 3 hours are needed:

  • Laptop: 120W/h consumption for 3 hours is 360W
  • Router: 15W/h consumption for 3 hours is 45W

The sum of these two is 405W over 3 hours. If we divide 405/3h, we get 135 W/h.

The pedal machine can generate up to 200 watts/hour at a medium pedaling speed and up to 400 watts/hour at a high speed.

Step 2 → Estimate the specifications of the complementary equipment.

Remember that to assemble any power generation system, you need these components:

An electricity generator This is any device capable of capturing and transforming some type of energy (mechanical, solar, thermal, hydraulic, chemical) into electrical energy. In this case, it’s the bicycle combined with an electric motor and a static stand, but we could also think of a gasoline generator, a solar panel, a Pelton turbine, a wind turbine, etc.

A charge controller Its function is to regulate the flow of energy from the motor to the batteries, controlling both current and voltage. This ensures that the recharge happens under optimal conditions, doesn’t damage the batteries, and extends their lifespan.

A battery It stores the generated energy in the form of chemical energy and releases it later as direct current in a controlled manner.

A power inverter It converts the direct current coming out of the battery into alternating current so we can plug in our equipment.

Solar cable and accessories This is an electrical cable robust enough to transmit the electrical energy we generate. Additionally, we can buy plastic connectors that help make the connections more secure so they don’t disconnect easily.

As you can see, you can use the same complementary system as you would for a solar panel setup. For this reason, we will calculate the equipment as if it were for a solar panel system, since they will have the same capacity.

Step 3 → How to estimate battery specifications?

We talked about an energy consumption of 405W over 3 hours, or 135 W/h. In addition to the total daily consumption, we need:

  • Days of autonomy (days): This refers to the days of use. If used every day, the autonomy is 5 or more days; if only used on weekends, we have 1 or 2 days. For our SOLE case, we will set it to a day and a half, 1.5.
  • Depth of discharge (DoD): For gel or AGM batteries, this value is 70%. For flooded lead-acid batteries, it is 50%.
  • Battery voltage (V): We choose this; since our devices are 12V, we will use that value.
  • Temperature and efficiency losses: We will assume 15%.

To calculate the battery capacity in Ampere-hours (Ah), we use the following formula: Ah = 1.15 * (Wh/day * days) / (DoD * V) = 1.15 * (135 * 1.5) / (70% * 12) = 27.72Ah. This is the minimum necessary capacity, so we decide to buy a 12V, 35Ah battery.


Step 4 → How to estimate controller specifications?

For this, we have to calculate the input and output current of the controller using these two equations:

To calculate the input current, we just have to multiply 3 values:

  • 1.25: A safety factor of 1.25. This is done to calculate with 25% more than necessary to avoid errors from a calculation that is too tight. It is common engineering practice to over-dimension calculations for safety parameters in case of failure.
  • Imod,sc: Unit current of the photovoltaic panel under short-circuit conditions, which can be found on the back of the panel. For this case, it is 2.32 A.

Np: number of panels in series. For this case, there are 2.

Calculating, we get: Iinput = 1.25 * 2.32 A * 2 = 5.8 A. To calculate the output current, we have to add, multiply, and divide the following values:

  • 1.25: A safety factor of 1.25.
  • Pdc: Direct current discharge power, which is the sum of the current of the devices we are going to connect that run on direct current. In this case, we have none, so it is 0.
  • Pac: Alternating current load power, which is the sum of the current of the devices we are going to connect that run on alternating current. In this case, a laptop consumes 120W/h and a router consumes 15W/h, for a total of 135 W/h.
  • Ninv: Inverter efficiency, around 90-95%.
  • Vbat: Battery voltage. We can read this on the selected battery. For this case, it is 12V.

Calculating, we get: Ioutput = 1.25 * (0 + (120 + 15) / 0.95) / 12 = 14.80 A. Finally, we just have to round our calculations. Iinput = 5.8 ≃ 6A. Ioutput = 14.80 ≃ 15A. So, our controller should support at least an input intensity of 6A and an output intensity of 15A. That’s why we bought a 20A one with a 12/24V voltage (it switches automatically).

Step 5 → How to estimate inverter specifications?

Since we are going to use low-consumption equipment, the inverter to use is a modified sine wave one. The calculation is done by over-dimensioning 25% above our total consumption. Remember that the total consumption is 405W. Thus, I = W * 1.25 = 405 * 1.25 = 506.25 W. This should be the minimum capacity of the inverter. In our case, we bought a 750W one.

Step 6 → How to know which cable to buy?

Usually, solar cables are sized using the AWG (American Wire Gauge) term to estimate the scale of the gauge. But for solar cables, the gauge is normally specified by its cross-section or area in mm². In Colombia, we can find gauges from 2.5mm² to 16mm² depending on the manufacturer.

It should be chosen in relation to the current to be transported. In this case, we will not transport more than 20A, so a 14 AWG cable, which is 2.5 mm², is fine. Regarding connectors, for our case, we bought two, but it will depend on the configuration each person builds.

Step 7 → Find a supplier

Look for an electrical supply provider and get a quote for this equipment. You can also ask them for advice to see if the calculations you made are correct or if there is any other way to optimize your power generation system. Compare costs between different suppliers and buy while keeping in mind the reliability of the place, the warranty, and the prices.

How to use it?

Precautions Now that we have the motor, the bicycle, the battery, and the inverter, we must remember that the battery is the last thing to be connected.

A safety warning: We should only connect one pole at a time so we don’t get shocked. It’s good to work with rubber gloves, as they insulate us against possible electrocution. We recommend reading the section Basic precautions when working with electricity.

Connecting the circuit The motor has a negative and a positive pole; it is important to identify which is which to build the new circuit. Generally, the positive is represented by red and the negative by black. In the case of connecting a motor, you must identify which way it spins when connected one way or the other; the motor must be connected so that it spins in the same direction the bicycle will spin, that is, “forward.” Remember that for this solution, what we are doing is running a motor in reverse; that is, by spinning the motor, an electromagnetic field will be created that will interact with the permanent magnets of the electric motor, generating an electric current.

Now, connect the negative and positive wires coming out of the motor to the controller; it is clearly marked on the controller where each wire connects. Then, cut two wires and strip their ends to make the connection to the battery.

Now connect these wires coming out of the controller to the battery, respecting the positive and negative connections.

When you have connected them, join the inverter wires to the battery, and that’s how you’ll have your system ready. Once the entire system is assembled, you will be able to plug the computer or the router into the inverter.

How to know if this solution works?

With this solution, you will manage to have electricity to power your SOLE; remember that this solution is calculated using the example of the electrical consumption of a router and a computer. If you want to have permanent electricity for more equipment, you must do your own calculations.

Other possible Internets

This solution only allows for energy independence for a router and a laptop for 3 hours at night, provided the panel has captured sunlight throughout the day. If you are using it during the day, this time can be extended, as the panel will be charging constantly while you use the electrical devices and discharge it.
